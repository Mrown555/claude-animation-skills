3.1 What is inventory?

Inventory is the stock of materials a firm holds to meet future demand. It includes raw materials, work-in-progress (WIP), finished goods and MRO supplies (maintenance, repair and operating items).

Why firms hold inventory: to meet anticipated demand, to decouple production stages, to protect against stock-outs, to take advantage of quantity discounts and to hedge against price increases.

Inventory costs.

  • Ordering cost (S): cost of placing and receiving one order (paperwork, transport, inspection). It does not depend on order size.
  • Holding (carrying) cost (H): cost of keeping one unit in stock for one year (storage, insurance, obsolescence, cost of capital).
  • Shortage cost: lost sales and goodwill when demand cannot be met.

Large orders mean fewer orders (low ordering cost) but more stock on hand (high holding cost). Small orders do the opposite. Inventory management balances these two costs.

3.2 Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model

Assumptions: demand is known and constant; lead time is known and constant; the whole order arrives at once; no quantity discounts; only ordering and holding costs are relevant; no shortages.

Let D = annual demand (units), S = ordering cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year, Q = order quantity.

Annual ordering cost = (D / Q) × S    Annual holding cost = (Q / 2) × H

Total annual cost: TC = (D/Q)·S + (Q/2)·H

Total cost is minimum where annual ordering cost equals annual holding cost. Solving gives

EOQ = Q* = √( 2DS / H )

Number of orders per year N = D / Q*    Time between orders = working days per year / N

3.3 Numerical example: EOQ

Example 1. A distributor sells 12,000 units of a product per year. The cost of placing one order is ₹500 and the holding cost is ₹30 per unit per year. The firm works 300 days per year. Find the EOQ, the number of orders per year, the time between orders and the total annual cost.

Solution. D = 12,000 units, S = ₹500, H = ₹30.

EOQ = √(2 × 12,000 × 500 / 30) = √400,000 = 632.46 ≈ 632 units

N = 12,000 / 632.46 = 18.97 ≈ 19 orders per year

Time between orders = 300 / 18.97 = 15.8 ≈ 16 working days

TC = (12,000 / 632.46) × 500 + (632.46 / 2) × 30 = 9,486.83 + 9,486.83 = ₹18,973.67

Note that at the EOQ the annual ordering cost equals the annual holding cost.

3.4 Reorder point (ROP) and safety stock

The reorder point is the inventory level at which a new order is placed.

ROP = d × L + SS

where d = daily demand, L = lead time in days and SS = safety stock (extra stock kept to protect against uncertainty in demand or lead time).

Example 2. For the distributor in Example 1, daily demand d = 12,000 / 300 = 40 units. The lead time is 5 days and the firm keeps a safety stock of 60 units.

ROP = 40 × 5 + 60 = 260 units. When stock falls to 260 units, a new order of 632 units is placed.

3.5 ABC analysis

ABC analysis classifies items by annual consumption value (annual demand × unit cost), following the Pareto principle.

Class% of items% of annual valueControl
A10–20%70–80%Tight control, frequent review
Babout 30%15–25%Moderate control
C50–60%5–10%Simple control, bulk ordering

Limitation: ABC looks only at value; a cheap item can still be critical (it may stop production), so firms combine ABC with VED (Vital, Essential, Desirable) analysis.

Summary and reading

  • EOQ balances ordering and holding cost: Q* = √(2DS/H).
  • ROP = d × L + SS tells you when to order.
  • ABC analysis tells you where to focus control.
